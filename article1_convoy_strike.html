$90 Million Cocaine Convoy Destroyed in Mexico as Cartel Supply Chain Unravels

Mexican authorities, backed by undisclosed US partners, intercepted and destroyed a major narcotics convoy before it could cross into American territory — seizing intelligence that has thrown a prominent logistics firm into the centre of a federal investigation and revealed the death of a senior cartel lieutenant.

Carlos Mendez & Sarah Okafor· Global Tribune — Mexico City / Washington Bureau· 13 April 2025

In what officials are describing as one of the most significant interdiction operations in recent memory, a convoy of narcotics-laden trucks was intercepted and destroyed on Sunday on a remote stretch of highway in northern Mexico, before the vehicles could reach the United States border. The strike, publicly attributed to Mexican armed forces, destroyed an estimated $90 million worth of cocaine — though sources familiar with the operation suggest the true scale of US involvement is considerably greater than acknowledged.

The trucks, which had been moving under the cover of a legitimate third-party logistics operation, were intercepted in a region of Mexico that falls outside the control of federal highway authorities. The route, rarely used by commercial traffic, had been identified by investigators as a preferred corridor for narcotics shipments precisely because of the limited law enforcement presence along it.

“The trailers recovered at the scene were not generic. They were traceable. And what they trace back to is going to be very uncomfortable for a lot of people in boardrooms right now.” — Senior DOJ official, speaking on condition of anonymity

Of particular concern to investigators is the identity of the firm whose trailers were used to transport the drugs. The company — a major North American logistics provider operating legitimate freight routes between Mexico, Canada, and the United States — has not been named publicly. However, the Department of Justice has confirmed that an active anti-money laundering investigation is now underway, with scrutiny falling on the firm’s Know Your Customer and AML compliance frameworks across all affiliated entities.

The political fallout from the operation has been swift and complicated. While the Mexican government moved quickly to claim credit for the interdiction, it simultaneously accused American partners of overreach, citing the involvement of at least one US military helicopter — described by witnesses as an MH-6 light utility aircraft — which was observed firing into a residential area near the convoy route. Seven civilians are reported to have died in the incident. The Mexican government has demanded a formal explanation from Washington, and American personnel operating in-country are now subject to significantly tightened operational restrictions.

Mexican officials, speaking at the UN, were quick to ask for answers about the MH-6 incident, calling for the US pilot to be immediately taken into Mexican custody, where he will await a trial for his crimes against the Mexican people. US officials are yet to respond, but sources inside the Pentagon have confirmed that there will be some disciplinary proceedings regarding the pilot's future, although they are yet to announce what sanctions would be available. Until then, the Mexican government has threatened to sanction US companies operating in Mexico until the issue is addressed.

Amid the political controversy, a significant intelligence find has gone relatively unnoticed in public reporting. Mexican ground forces pushing into the area following the convoy strike recovered the body of a man identified as Lieutenant Jero Prieto, known within cartel networks by the alias el corredor — the runner — a reference to his role managing the physical movement of narcotics across the country. Prieto was a mid-to-senior figure in the organisation and his death represents a meaningful blow to the cartel’s operational middle layer.

More significant than his death, however, may be what he was carrying. US liaison officers embedded with Mexican forces intercepted documents recovered from Prieto’s body before they could be processed through standard Mexican federal channels. Those documents, now in the hands of US investigators, reportedly reference a location described only as Isla Nueva — a tourist destination off the Gulf Coast of Mexico — in the context of what appears to be a major undisclosed logistics operation. The nature of that operation is not yet confirmed, but federal sources say the reference has triggered an immediate and high-priority investigation.

⚑ INTELLIGENCE NOTE — DOCUMENTS RECOVERED FROM SCENE

Papers intercepted from a deceased individual at the convoy site make reference to “Isla Nueva” in the context of transit logistics and product holding. The documents have been forwarded to senior US federal authorities. Their full contents have not been disclosed publicly.

The operation has exposed a significant gap between the public narrative and the operational reality of US involvement in Mexican counter-narcotics efforts. For the Mexican government, the pressure is twofold: explain the civilian deaths linked to American air support, while simultaneously managing the revelation that a major logistics firm operating within its borders may have been knowingly or unknowingly facilitating one of the most significant drug supply chains in the hemisphere.

For Washington, the prize is the intelligence. And if the documents recovered from Prieto’s body are as significant as sources suggest, what happened in the desert on Sunday may prove to be only the opening move.
